    Geopolitical Risk Analysis
    Fragmented Efforts to Secure Strait of Hormuz Passage
    Sustain Risks for Businesses and Energy Markets
                                                              March 10, 2026


Bottom Line Up Front
   The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed to most commercial shipping
    since Iran threatened to attack transiting vessels, and subsequent assaults on at least
    ten ships have made that threat credible for operators, insurers, and charterers. Maritime
    traffic remains far below normal levels, falling from roughly 100 daily vessel transits before the war to only a
    handful per day, mostly linked to Iran, Russia, China, and Pakistan.
   The current crisis has also contributed to storage and export bottlenecks across regional energy infrastructure,
    forcing some production facilities to shut down and delaying or complicating eventual restart. In the first half of
    2025, the Strait of Hormuz carried about 21 million barrels per day, equivalent to roughly 20 percent of global
    petroleum liquids consumption and around one-quarter of global maritime oil trade.
   Governments and operators have floated a fragmented set of initiatives to restart secure passage, including
    proposed U.S. naval escorts and war-risk support, France’s post-conflict multinational escort concept, Pakistan’s
    protection mission for its own shipping, and ad hoc vessel tactics such as signaling Chinese links to reduce
    perceived targeting risk. These measures remain selective and insufficient to restore commercial confidence.



Risks and Vulnerabilities Summary
   Energy markets remain highly exposed to any continued disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude surged
    above US$100 on March 9 before falling back toward the low-US$90s on March 10 after U.S. President Donald
    Trump suggested the war could end soon, showing that prices may ease on optimism but will likely remain
    vulnerable unless the transit problem is credibly resolved.
   The disruption extends beyond crude exports and affects wider freight flows to and from the Gulf. Container
    shipping has also been severely disrupted, with carriers suspending services, invoking force majeure, and leaving
    regional cargo movements delayed or stranded, which raises costs and delivery uncertainty for non-energy
    businesses as well.
   Insurance costs and availability remain a central commercial constraint even if some escorted sailings resume.
    War-risk coverage has been canceled or severely restricted for Gulf transits, and market analysis indicates that
    even a U.S. insurance backstop may not be enough on its own to restore broad commercial navigation.
   Fragmented escort initiatives remain too uneven and selective to restore commercial confidence, leaving energy
    importers, traders, shippers, Gulf-linked manufacturers, and firms with Gulf operations exposed to continued
    disruption in insurance, freight, and energy supply.
   Over the longer term, the inability of Western and Gulf states to organize a credible common security framework
    for Hormuz could create strategic commercial distortions. If Pakistan protects only its own shipping, while
    Russia- or China-linked vessels benefit from their political relationships with Iran or from perceived immunity,
    access to the strait could become more unequal, politicized, and commercially distorted in ways that
    disadvantage Western firms and their partners.
   A widening of the crisis to the Red Sea would compound the shock to global trade. If the Houthis intensify threats
    to Red Sea shipping while Hormuz remains constrained, businesses would face a dual-chokepoint disruption
    affecting both Gulf exports and the main Europe-Asia maritime corridor, although that same escalation could
    also increase pressure for a more coordinated multinational response.

Outlook and Assessment

   Energy prices will likely remain elevated in the coming days because short-term political messaging is unlikely
    to outweigh the continued disruption or perceived blockage risk at the Strait of Hormuz.
    o   Confidence: High. Markets are still pricing the material drivers of risk, namely continued military activity,
        uncertainty over when the war will end, and the unresolved threat to traffic through Hormuz, rather than
        political signaling alone. The sharp drop in oil prices after President Trump’s remarks on March 10 shows
        headlines can move prices briefly, but Iranian messaging asserting that Tehran will define the war’s end,
        together with ongoing violence, suggests real-world disruption risk will remain the stronger driver in the
        near term. The main uncertainty is whether a sudden ceasefire or credible reopening mechanism emerges
        faster than currently appears likely.
   Before the war ends, Iran may seek a short, sharper escalation around the Strait of Hormuz, including additional
    attacks on anchored, drifting, or waiting commercial vessels, if it judges that current disruption is no longer
    sufficient to maintain coercive pressure.
    o   Confidence: Medium. This judgment is based on the logic that Iran has already used Hormuz disruption as
        a coercive lever, and that simply sustaining the current threat environment may become less effective if
        limited shipping begins to resume or if outside powers organize ad hoc escorts. Historical precedent from
        the 1980s Tanker War supports the risk of further maritime escalation in the Gulf, and current reporting
        already points to large concentrations of waiting tankers and continued aggressive Iranian signaling around
        the strait. Confidence is only medium because Iran’s next move is highly contingent on battlefield dynamics,
        internal decision-making, and the risk that attacking more ships could trigger a broader multinational
        intervention that Tehran may prefer to avoid.
   As the war continues in the coming days, additional patchwork and selective naval escort initiatives are likely to
    emerge alongside Pakistan’s mission, potentially including narrow coalition efforts focused on moving priority
    cargoes such as strategic oil exports or essential supplies for Gulf states rather than restoring broad commercial
    access.
    o   Confidence: Medium. The supporting factors of this judgement are the strong incentive to limit further oil-
        price increases, political pressure on leaders such as President Trump and President Macron to match public
        statements with visible action, and the fact that the United States and partners already maintain naval assets
        in and around the Gulf. Confidence remains medium because current proposals are still fragmented, industry
        skepticism remains high, and there is limited evidence so far that governments are prepared to implement
        the kind of broad, sustained escort architecture that would restore normal commercial traffic rather than
        protect only selected shipments.
   Key indicators to watch are U.S. statements that core war aims have been achieved or are within reach, the
    formation of naval escort missions, any Iranian escalation against anchored or waiting ships around the Strait of
    Hormuz, and signs of diplomatic de-escalation. Any of these could indicate either a path toward selective
    reopening or a sharper escalation in maritime risk.
   In practical terms, business leaders should plan for continued volatility rather than a rapid return to normal
    operations: oil and freight costs are likely to stay elevated, insurance and routing disruptions may persist, and
    any reopening of traffic through the Strait of Hormuz in the coming two to four weeks is more likely to be
    selective and reversible than broad-based. Companies with exposure to Gulf energy, shipping, procurement, or
    regional operations should therefore prioritize contingency sourcing, freight flexibility, and short-cycle decision-
    making over assumptions that political reassurance alone will stabilize conditions.
